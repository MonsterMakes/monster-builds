Base plate

M4, rod nuts and washers

M4 is the plate the foot of the upper stair stands on: bearing on the existing pier over flashing, held down by the four existing rods, with the M1 level cut and the M2b tread 1 stub welded to its top.

Field measurement: rod holes only, drilled at dry fit to the rods as set HOLD. The plate can be cut now. M4 is not shimmed; the pitch and cross slope it takes on the pier go into the M1 level cut in part 3.

Notes, concerns and callouts

The washer runs into the stub weld. Unresolved. The uphill rod row sits 2-5/8 each side of centre and the M2b stub face is at 2, so a 1-1/4 OD washer reaches exactly to the stub and then overlaps its 1/4 fillet by 1/4. A standard nut lands almost on the fillet, with no socket clearance and little for a wrench. Options: a smaller washer with a flat ground on the stub side, a lighter fillet local to the rod, or setting and torquing those two nuts before the stub is welded on. Decide before the stub goes on M4; after that the third option is gone.

The M2b notch cannot be cut in the shop. Its depth is whatever the trimmed M1 level cut leaves, and nobody knows that until M4 is on the pier HOLD. Cut, weld and grind it at dry fit before coating.

Wall plate

M6, anchors

M6 is the plate the head of the upper stair hangs on: bolted flat to the concrete wall on six adhesive anchors, with the stringer's plumb cut welded to its face. It carries the whole upper end of the flight.

Field measurement: the plate is fixed and can be cut and drilled now. Where the anchors land waits on a rebar scan HOLD. The wall's lean and plan angle are measured here too, but they change the M1 plumb cut in part 3, not this plate.

Notes, concerns and callouts

Embed 3-1/2 minimum, 4-1/2 maximum. The stem is 6 thick, so embedment is bounded at both ends: deeper is stronger, since bond grows with depth and breakout with depth to the 1.5 power, but the hole has to stop short of the back face and clear of the bar layer, which sits about 4 in from the front. 4-1/2 is the practical ceiling. Drill 9/16 for the 1/2 rod, the same bit as the plate holes, with a carbide hammer bit. Brush and blow each hole clean and dry before the adhesive: a hole full of dust is the usual reason an adhesive anchor underperforms.

1-1/2 minimum edge distance, and it is my addition. The drawing set says to shift the anchors clear of the wall verticals but never says how far they may move. The nominal pattern leaves 1-1/2 to the plate sides and 2 top and bottom. If the scan forces a hole closer than 1-1/2 to any edge, the plate grows rather than the edge distance shrinking.

3

Stringer weldment

M1, M2a stubs, M2b tread-1 stub, stub holes, weep

The backbone: one length of HSS 8×4×1/4 cut level at the base and plumb at the wall, with six stubs welded along its top face and the two plates welded to its ends. Everything else in the stair hangs off this weldment, and almost every dimension on it comes from the field.

Field measurement: nearly all of it HOLD. The total rise and run set the length, both cut angles and every stub mark. The pier's pitch and cross slope trim the level cut; the wall's lean and plan angle trim the plumb cut. Cut nothing until the readings from parts 1 and 2 are in hand.

Variants

Three: the upper stair as drawn here, the lower stairs, and the master bath stair. Same section, same stub, same connection; the length, the cut angles and the stub marks come from each flight's own geometry.

Hardware

None. M1 carries no fasteners of its own: the treads bolt to the collars in part 4, the plates are welded, and the anchors and rods belong to parts 1 and 2.

Notes, concerns and callouts

Drill the vent holes before the stubs go on. Each stub needs a 3/16 hole through the stringer's top face, its downhill edge flush with the inside of the stub's downhill wall, so the stub drains and vents into the stringer. Once the stub is welded down, that hole is inside a closed box and cannot be drilled. A stub welded shut with no vent can blow its weld in the cure oven.

One stub angle, and it moves with the rise. All six stubs share a single 31.43° cut, which is the stringer's slope. That angle comes from the total rise and run recorded in parts 1 and 2 HOLD. If either changes, the angle, both stub face lengths and every stub mark along the tube change with it. Cut one stub, check it on the slope jig, then cut the rest.

The weep notch is cut last. A 3/16 notch in the bottom edge of M1 at whichever corner sits lowest on M4 as set HOLD. Which corner that is depends on the pier's cross slope, so it cannot be chosen in the shop. Tape it and the stub holes while blasting, pull the tape before coating, and leave everything open afterwards.

4

Tread frame

F1, F2, F3, M8 collars, M3 plate, P1 stems, screw holes

The steel frame under each tread: a picture frame of HSS laid flat, two joists, and a centre connection of two collars with the M3 plate between them. It drops over its stub, bears on the stub through a neoprene pad under M3, and is held by two through bolts. The ash screws on from below at the bench. Post treads also carry the P1 stems their guard posts sleeve over.

Field measurement: none for the frame itself. Every dimension here is fixed, so all seven can be built before the survey. Only the through-bolt holes wait, and they are drilled at dry fit onto the actual stub HOLD.

Notes, concerns and callouts

Drill with the pad in the joint. The frame bears on the pad, so the pad sets how high it rides on the stub. Drill the bolt holes with the real pad in place, or an offcut of it, and slot the collar holes 13/32 × 9/16 vertically HOLD. Drilled dry and round, every hole ends up 1/16 out, and the clearance on a 3/8 bolt is 1/32 — the bolt will not start.

Two different F2 sections, and they are not interchangeable. Post treads take the 3 wide end member so the stem has a landing; plain treads take 2-1/2. The F1 short point differs to match, 42 against 43. A 2-1/2 end member on a post tread leaves the stem overhanging its own support, and the miters will not close.

Once drilled, a tread belongs to its stub. The holes are bored through collars and stub together, so the frames stop being interchangeable at that moment. Stamp each frame and its stub before anything comes apart for coating.

Spigots

Plate, stem, rods

How a guard post meets concrete. A plate bolted down with adhesive rods, a stem welded to it, and the post sleeved over the stem and through-bolted. Two variants: the single-rod wall spigot used under every level and raked post on the wall or a pier, and the wider two-rod plate under a gate hinge post.

Field measurement: the parts are fixed and can be built now. Where each rod lands is not: the wall is scanned at every spigot and the hole set between the verticals HOLD.

Notes, concerns and callouts

Shim before the nut goes on, not after. The plate levels on horseshoe shims slipped around the rod, then beds in adhesive. Torque the nut onto an unshimmed plate and a 3/8 plate will bend to the concrete rather than the concrete yielding to it, and the stem goes out of plumb with it. Shim first, adhesive, then torque.

Three cable holes pass through the stem, and they are oversize there. Measured from the top of the plate the grid lands at 2-11/16, 5-13/16 and 8-15/16, all inside the 10 stem. The post holes are 7/16; the stem holes are 9/16, so a cable slightly out of line with the stem still passes without bearing on it. Drill post and stem together with the post on the stem; drilled apart they will not line up at all.

The hinge post plate is not a bigger version of the other one. Its two rods run along the wall, so the plate resists the gate leaf trying to twist the post about the wall line. Turn that plate 90° and the rods sit in line with the load, which is the one orientation that does nothing.

6 of embedment, and the wall is 6 thick. Unlike the wall plate in part 2, these rods are specified at full depth. They sit on the wall centreline rather than in its face, so the cone is not cut short by a free edge, but it does mean the hole is nearly through the stem HOLD: scan first, and stop the bit short of the far face.
